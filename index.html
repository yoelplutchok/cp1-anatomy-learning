Where the airway comes from: lung bud and germ layers

L1Learning
What is the first sign of the developing respiratory system?
  1. A. The tracheo-oesophageal septum
  2. B. The laryngotracheal diverticulum
  3. C. The hypopharyngeal eminence
AnswerB. The laryngotracheal diverticulum
The laryngotracheal diverticulum (respiratory diverticulum, lung bud) is the first sign of the respiratory system: a pouch growing out of the foregut around day 28.
Explanation

The laryngotracheal diverticulum is a pouch of foregut endoderm, with its coat of splanchnic mesoderm, that grows out of the floor of the primordial pharynx in week 4. It is also called the respiratory diverticulum or lung bud. The larynx, trachea, bronchi and lungs all grow from it.

  • The tracheo-oesophageal septum comes afterwards: folds on either side of the diverticulum fuse to wall it off from the oesophagus.
  • The hypopharyngeal eminence is a swelling in the pharyngeal floor that forms the back of the tongue. It is a landmark beside the laryngotracheal groove, not the start of the airway.

L2Learning
Which germ layer forms the epithelial lining and the glands of the larynx, trachea and bronchi?
  1. A. Surface ectoderm
  2. B. Endoderm
  3. C. Splanchnic mesoderm
AnswerB. Endoderm
The lining comes from the endoderm of the foregut diverticulum, so airway epithelium and glands are endodermal, like the gut lining.
Explanation

The respiratory diverticulum is an outgrowth of foregut endoderm. Everything that grows from that endodermal tube is endodermal: the pseudostratified ciliated epithelium with goblet cells of the trachea and bronchi, the submucosal glands (which bud from the epithelium) and, further down, the alveolar epithelium.

  • Splanchnic mesoderm wraps around the tube and forms the wall: cartilage, smooth muscle and connective tissue.
  • Surface ectoderm lines the mouth and nasal cavity, not the lower airway.

Hook: lining = endoderm; everything around it = mesoderm.

L3Learning
What do the laryngeal muscles and cartilages develop from, and which nerve supplies the larynx as a result?
  1. A. Mesoderm of pharyngeal arches 1 and 2 → trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V)
  2. B. Mesoderm of pharyngeal arch 3 → glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve IX)
  3. C. Mesoderm of pharyngeal arches 4 and 6 → vagus nerve (cranial nerve X)
AnswerC. Mesoderm of pharyngeal arches 4 and 6 → vagus nerve (cranial nerve X)
Laryngeal muscles and cartilages come from arches 4 and 6, whose nerve is the vagus (CN X): superior laryngeal (arch 4) and recurrent laryngeal (arch 6).
Explanation

Unlike the trachea, whose wall comes from splanchnic mesoderm, the larynx is built from pharyngeal arch mesoderm. Each arch brings its own nerve, so muscles from arches 4 and 6 are supplied by branches of the vagus: the superior laryngeal nerve (arch 4, cricothyroid) and the recurrent laryngeal nerve (arch 6, the other intrinsic laryngeal muscles).

  • Arch 3 forms the stylopharyngeus, supplied by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX).
  • Arches 1 and 2 give the muscles of mastication (trigeminal, CN V) and of facial expression (facial, CN VII).

Splitting the airway from the oesophagus

L4Learning
How does the tracheo-oesophageal septum form?
  1. A. The foregut lumen fills with an epithelial plug that later recanalises
  2. B. Tracheo-oesophageal folds grow in from each side and fuse in the midline
  3. C. A single fold grows down from the roof of the pharynx and fuses with its floor
AnswerB. Tracheo-oesophageal folds grow in from each side and fuse in the midline
Longitudinal tracheo-oesophageal folds (ridges) appear on both sides of the diverticulum and fuse into the septum.
Explanation

The tracheo-oesophageal folds are ridges that bulge into the foregut lumen from its two sides. As they grow toward each other and fuse, they form the tracheo-oesophageal septum. The tube in front becomes the laryngotracheal tube; the one behind becomes the oesophagus.

  • Epithelial plug and recanalisation is how the oesophagus later restores its own lumen; incomplete recanalisation gives oesophageal stenosis, not a septum.
  • The folds come from the sides, not from the roof. That is why the slides show them in cross-section as two indentations meeting in the middle.

Tracheo-oesophageal fistula and oesophageal atresia

L5Learning
What is the commonest variety of tracheo-oesophageal fistula (about 85%)?
  1. A. H-type fistula between a continuous trachea and oesophagus, with no atresia
  2. B. Oesophageal atresia with a blind lower pouch + a fistula from the trachea to the upper oesophagus
  3. C. Oesophageal atresia with a blind upper pouch + a fistula from the trachea to the lower oesophagus
AnswerC. Oesophageal atresia with a blind upper pouch + a fistula from the trachea to the lower oesophagus
About 85% of cases are atresia with a distal fistula: blind upper pouch, and the lower oesophagus opening into the trachea.
Explanation

In this commonest variety (type C in the gross classification on the slide, about 82–87%) the upper oesophagus ends blindly, and the lower oesophagus arises from the trachea near the carina. Saliva and milk pool in the pouch and spill into the larynx; air passes from the trachea into the stomach; gastric juice can reflux up into the lungs.

  • A fistula from the upper pouch alone is rare (about 1–4%).
  • H-type (fistula without atresia) is about 4%.

Remaining varieties: isolated atresia (about 8%) and fistulas at both ends (rare).

L6Learning
Why does a pregnancy with oesophageal atresia often show polyhydramnios?
  1. A. The fetus inhales amniotic fluid into its lungs instead of swallowing it
  2. B. The fetal kidneys produce excess urine
  3. C. The fetus cannot swallow amniotic fluid into its gut
AnswerC. The fetus cannot swallow amniotic fluid into its gut
Amniotic fluid is normally cleared by fetal swallowing and absorption in the gut. With atresia it cannot pass, while urine keeps adding fluid: polyhydramnios.
Explanation

Polyhydramnios is an excess of amniotic fluid. The fetus keeps passing urine into the amniotic cavity, and normally swallows the fluid, absorbs it in the gut and passes it via the placenta to the mother. A blind oesophagus breaks this loop, so fluid builds up.

  • Fetal breathing movements move fluid in and out of the lungs, but the lungs cannot take over the job of removing it; if anything they add their own secreted fluid to the amniotic cavity.
  • Urine output is normal; it is removal that fails. The opposite problem, no urine (bilateral renal agenesis), gives oligohydramnios.

Bronchial buds, lobes and pleura

L7Learning
Which bronchial buds correspond to the lobes of the lungs?
  1. A. Primary bronchial buds: 3 on the right, 2 on the left
  2. B. Secondary bronchial buds: 3 on the right, 2 on the left
  3. C. Secondary bronchial buds: 2 on the right, 3 on the left
AnswerB. Secondary bronchial buds: 3 on the right, 2 on the left
The secondary (lobar) bronchial buds, 3 right and 2 left, give the right upper/middle/lower and left upper/lower lobes.
Explanation

Branching runs: respiratory bud → 2 primary buds (main bronchi) → secondary buds (lobar bronchi: 3 right, 2 left, hence 3 and 2 lobes) → tertiary buds (segmental bronchi: 10 right, 8 left).

  • 2 right, 3 left swaps the sides: the right lung is the one with the middle lobe.
  • There are only two primary buds, one per lung.

The older slide calls the lobar branches "3 main bronchi on the right, 2 on the left"; anatomically, "main" bronchi are the two primary ones.

Stages of lung maturation: timing and landmarks

L8Learning
What are the four classic stages of lung maturation, in order, with their timing?
  1. A. Pseudoglandular 6–16 wk → canalicular 16–26 wk → terminal sac 26 wk–birth → alveolar 32 wk–8 yr
  2. B. Pseudoglandular 6–16 wk → terminal sac 16–26 wk → canalicular 26 wk–birth → alveolar 32 wk–8 yr
  3. C. Canalicular 6–16 wk → pseudoglandular 16–26 wk → terminal sac 26 wk–birth → alveolar 32 wk–8 yr
AnswerA. Pseudoglandular 6–16 wk → canalicular 16–26 wk → terminal sac 26 wk–birth → alveolar 32 wk–8 yr
Pseudoglandular 6–16 wk, Canalicular 16–26 wk, Terminal sac 26 wk–birth, Alveolar 32 wk–8 yr.
Explanation

The stages follow the airway outward: first the conducting tubes (pseudoglandular), then the respiratory bronchioles and a blood supply (canalicular), then thin-walled terminal sacs (terminal sac), then mature alveoli (alveolar). The alveolar stage overlaps the terminal sac stage from 32 weeks.

  • The current lecture adds an embryonic stage (weeks 4–5) before these four. Newer textbooks give overlapping windows: about 5–17, 16–25, 24 weeks to late fetal life, and late fetal life to 8 years.
  • The wrong orders swap canalicular with its neighbours.

Hook: "Please Call The Alveoli".

Late stages: terminal sacs, pneumocytes and alveoli

L9Learning
Which two things must both be in place for a premature baby to be viable?
  1. A. A thick enough blood–air barrier
    Enough surfactant to stop alveoli overinflating
  2. B. A thin enough blood–air barrier
    Enough fetal haemoglobin to load oxygen
  3. C. A thin enough blood–air barrier
    Enough surfactant to stop alveoli collapsing
AnswerC. A thin enough blood–air barrier
Enough surfactant to stop alveoli collapsing
Viability needs a thin blood–air barrier for gas exchange and enough surfactant to keep the alveoli from collapsing.
Explanation

Both depend on the terminal sac stage: the type I cells and capillaries must be close enough for diffusion, and the type II cells must make enough surfactant to keep the small air spaces open at the end of each breath. Delivery before surfactant is adequate causes neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

  • Fetal haemoglobin is present throughout fetal life; it is not the limiting factor.
  • The barrier must be thin, not thick, and surfactant prevents collapse, not overinflation.

Surfactant and neonatal respiratory distress syndrome

L10Learning
Which cells make surfactant, and what does it do?
  1. A. Type II pneumocytes; it raises surface tension in the alveoli
  2. B. Type I pneumocytes; it lowers surface tension in the alveoli
  3. C. Type II pneumocytes; it lowers surface tension in the alveoli
AnswerC. Type II pneumocytes; it lowers surface tension in the alveoli
Type II pneumocytes secrete surfactant into the alveolar space, where it lowers surface tension and prevents collapse at the end of expiration.
Explanation

Surfactant is a phospholipid–protein film (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) stored in the lamellar bodies of type II cells and secreted onto the fluid lining the alveoli. By lowering surface tension it keeps the alveoli open during exhalation and makes them easy to inflate.

  • Raising surface tension would favour collapse, the opposite of its job.
  • Type I cells are thin gas-exchange cells and do not secrete surfactant.

What is given to the mother before an expected preterm delivery to speed up fetal surfactant production?
  1. A. Insulin, to keep fetal blood glucose low
  2. B. A glucocorticoid such as dexamethasone or betamethasone
  3. C. Oestrogen, to speed placental maturation
AnswerB. A glucocorticoid such as dexamethasone or betamethasone
Antenatal glucocorticoids (dexamethasone or betamethasone) given to the mother before preterm delivery boost fetal surfactant synthesis and reduce RDS.
Explanation

Corticosteroids cross the placenta and accelerate fetal lung maturation. They are given when preterm delivery cannot be stopped, for example after preterm labour, rupture of membranes or a low L:S ratio.

  • Oestrogen is not used to mature the fetal lungs.

Fetal breathing and the switch at birth

L12Learning
What do fetal breathing movements do?
  1. A. Draw amniotic fluid into the lungs → supply nutrients to the developing lung epithelium
  2. B. Draw amniotic fluid into the lungs → condition the breathing muscles and stimulate lung maturation
  3. C. Expel amniotic fluid from the lungs → condition the breathing muscles and stimulate lung maturation
AnswerB. Draw amniotic fluid into the lungs → condition the breathing muscles and stimulate lung maturation
The fetus "practises" breathing: it aspirates amniotic fluid, which conditions the breathing muscles and stimulates lung maturation.
Explanation

Fetal breathing movements happen before birth. Moving fluid in and out keeps the lungs distended and trains the diaphragm and other respiratory muscles. Lungs that cannot be distended by fluid (for example with oligohydramnios) do not grow normally.

  • The key event is drawing fluid in; the lungs are about half full of fluid by birth.
  • The fluid's role is mechanical (distension) and training, not nutrition.

Pulmonary hypoplasia

L13Learning
How does bilateral renal agenesis cause pulmonary hypoplasia?
  1. A. No fetal urine → urea builds up in amniotic fluid → type II cells destroyed
  2. B. No fetal urine → oligohydramnios → lungs not distended and thorax squeezed by the uterus
  3. C. No fetal urine → oligohydramnios → lungs lack growth factors made by the kidneys
AnswerB. No fetal urine → oligohydramnios → lungs not distended and thorax squeezed by the uterus
Oligohydramnios leaves the lungs without fluid to distend them and lets the uterine wall compress the small thorax, so the lungs cannot grow.
Explanation

Without kidneys the fetus makes no urine, so amniotic fluid volume falls (oligohydramnios). Lack of fluid restricts the size of the fetal thorax, the uterine walls press directly on it, and fetal breathing movements cannot fill the lungs. Lung growth stops short; the infant may die of respiratory failure at birth.

  • The lungs do not depend on a kidney-derived growth factor; the mechanism is mechanical.
  • Urea does not destroy type II cells; the problem is lung size, not surfactant.

Congenital diaphragmatic hernia

L15Learning
A newborn has severe respiratory distress, a scaphoid abdomen and bowel sounds over the left chest; X-ray shows bowel loops in the left hemithorax. A defect in which structure caused this?
  1. A. Dorsal mesentery of the oesophagus
  2. B. Left pleuroperitoneal membrane
  3. C. Septum transversum
AnswerB. Left pleuroperitoneal membrane
This is a congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH): the left pleuroperitoneal membrane failed to close, leaving a posterolateral opening.
Explanation

If a pleuroperitoneal membrane fails to close its canal, abdominal organs can slide up into the chest when the intestines return to the abdomen. Bowel sounds are then heard in the chest, the abdomen looks sunken (scaphoid) because its contents have moved up, and the compressed lung cannot ventilate.

  • A septum transversum defect would affect the central tendon: a rare central or anterior defect.
  • The dorsal mesentery of the oesophagus forms the crura; it is not the usual site.

In a left congenital diaphragmatic hernia, which way is the heart displaced, compared with a hypoplastic lung of other cause?
  1. A. Toward the hernia (to the left), exactly as with a hypoplastic lung of other cause

  • The two conditions shift the mediastinum in opposite directions; that contrast is the exam point.

Where the airway comes from: lung bud and germ layers

L17Learning
From which part of the gut tube does the lung bud arise?
  1. A. The ventral wall of the foregut
  2. B. The dorsal wall of the foregut
  3. C. The ventral wall of the hindgut
AnswerA. The ventral wall of the foregut
The respiratory diverticulum buds from the ventral wall of the foregut, which is why the trachea lies in front of (ventral to) the oesophagus.
Explanation

The foregut is the cranial part of the gut tube formed by embryonic folding; its first part is the primordial pharynx. The respiratory diverticulum pushes out of its ventral (front) wall into the surrounding splanchnic mesoderm. Its distal end swells into the lung bud, and its proximal part becomes the trachea.

  • Dorsal wall of the foregut: the dorsal part of the partitioned foregut becomes the oesophagus, not the airway.
  • Hindgut: the caudal end of the gut tube (distal colon, rectum) has nothing to do with the lungs.

Hook: airway in front, food tube behind, in the embryo as in the adult.

What forms the cartilage, smooth muscle and connective tissue of the trachea and bronchi?

Splanchnic mesoderm around the foregut forms the airway wall: cartilage, smooth muscle and connective tissue, plus vessels and visceral pleura.

Tracheo-oesophageal fistula and oesophageal atresia

L19Learning
What developmental error causes tracheo-oesophageal fistula with oesophageal atresia?
  1. A. Failure of the pleuroperitoneal membranes to fuse
  2. B. Incomplete fusion of the tracheo-oesophageal folds (faulty septum)
  3. C. Incomplete recanalisation of the oesophagus after its plug
AnswerB. Incomplete fusion of the tracheo-oesophageal folds (faulty septum)
TEF and oesophageal atresia come from faulty partitioning: the tracheo-oesophageal folds fail to fuse fully, so the septum leaves trachea and oesophagus connected.
Explanation

A fistula is an abnormal passage between two organs. In TEF the trachea and oesophagus stay connected because the tracheo-oesophageal septum is incomplete (standard texts describe it deviating posteriorly). The same faulty partition usually leaves the oesophagus interrupted (oesophageal atresia), which is why the two so often occur together.

  • Incomplete recanalisation of the oesophagus gives oesophageal stenosis: a narrowed lumen with no connection to the airway.
  • Pleuroperitoneal membrane failure gives a congenital diaphragmatic hernia.

A newborn with oesophageal atresia has gas in the stomach on an abdominal X-ray. What does the gas show?
  1. A. A fistula connects the trachea to the lower oesophagus
  2. B. A fistula connects the trachea to the blind upper pouch only
  3. C. Swallowed air passes through the blind upper pouch to the stomach
AnswerA. A fistula connects the trachea to the lower oesophagus
With atresia nothing swallowed reaches the stomach, so stomach gas must come from the airway through a distal fistula (trachea → lower oesophagus).
Explanation

Every breath and every cry pushes air from the trachea through the fistula into the lower oesophagus and stomach, so the stomach and bowel contain gas and may be distended. This is the commonest variety: blind upper pouch + distal fistula.

  • A blind pouch passes nothing down; that is what atresia means.

Rule: atresia + gas in the stomach = distal fistula. Atresia + gasless abdomen = isolated atresia or a proximal fistula only.

Bronchial buds, lobes and pleura

L22Learning
What drives the repeated branching of the bronchial buds into the bronchial tree?
  1. A. The endoderm of the buds on its own; the surrounding tissue only wraps the finished tubes
  2. B. The surrounding splanchnic mesoderm (connective tissue), which signals the endodermal buds to divide
  3. C. The somatic mesoderm of the body wall, which the buds press against as they grow
AnswerB. The surrounding splanchnic mesoderm (connective tissue), which signals the endodermal buds to divide
Branching is driven by the splanchnic mesoderm (connective tissue) around the buds: its signals make the endodermal buds divide again and again.
Explanation

The bronchial tree is built by an epithelial–mesenchymal interaction. The endodermal bud supplies the tube, but it branches only where the surrounding splanchnic mesoderm (the future connective tissue, cartilage and smooth muscle of the airway wall) signals it to; these signals include members of the fibroblast growth factor (FGF) family. The review slide puts it as “branching occurs via septation from connective tissue in the walls”: the connective tissue around and between the buds splits each tip into new branches. Each segmental bronchus with its own mass of mesenchyme becomes a bronchopulmonary segment.

  • Endoderm on its own does not branch: stripped of its mesoderm, the bud stops dividing.
  • Somatic mesoderm lines the body wall and forms only the parietal pleura; the pleural cavity separates it from the growing buds.

Stages of lung maturation: timing and landmarks

L23Learning
What forms in the pseudoglandular stage (6–16 weeks)?
  1. A. Simple columnar endodermal tubes branch repeatedly down to terminal bronchioles; few capillaries
  2. B. Terminal sacs bud from alveolar ducts; capillaries bulge into them
  3. C. Cuboidal tubes form respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts; rich capillaries
AnswerA. Simple columnar endodermal tubes branch repeatedly down to terminal bronchioles; few capillaries
Pseudoglandular: endodermal tubes of simple columnar epithelium branch repeatedly (about 15–25 rounds) down to the terminal bronchioles; only some capillaries. The lung looks like a gland.
Explanation

Under the microscope the lung resembles an exocrine gland: blind-ending epithelial tubes in loose mesenchyme with few capillaries. All the conducting airways form here. The lecture's "15–25 terminal bronchioles" counts rounds (generations) of branching, not individual bronchioles: the finished conducting tree ends in tens of thousands of terminal bronchioles. The current slide adds that the epithelium starts to differentiate (pseudostratified ciliated columnar cells, goblet cells).

  • Respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts with rich capillaries describe the canalicular stage.
  • Terminal sacs with bulging capillaries describe the terminal sac stage.

Late stages: terminal sacs, pneumocytes and alveoli

L25Learning
In which stage do type II pneumocytes start producing surfactant, according to the current lecture?
  1. A. Canalicular stage (16–26 weeks)
  2. B. Alveolar stage (32 weeks–8 years)
  3. C. Pseudoglandular stage (6–16 weeks)
AnswerA. Canalicular stage (16–26 weeks)
The current lecture puts the start of surfactant production by type II pneumocytes in the canalicular stage (from about 20 weeks); amounts then rise.
Explanation

The first type II cells and the first small amounts of surfactant appear late in the canalicular stage (standard texts: by about 20 weeks). A 25-week preterm baby therefore has some surfactant, just not enough. Output rises through the terminal sac stage and especially from about 32–34 weeks.

  • Pseudoglandular lungs have only conducting tubes; there is no respiratory epithelium to make surfactant.

The older lecture places the differentiation of the cuboidal cells into type I and type II pneumocytes in the terminal sac stage; both fit, because differentiation starts at the end of the canalicular stage and most of it happens in the terminal sac stage.

Surfactant and neonatal respiratory distress syndrome

L26Learning
Which amniotic-fluid result indicates that the fetal lungs are mature?
  1. A. A lecithin:sphingomyelin ratio below 2
  2. B. A sphingomyelin:lecithin ratio above 2
  3. C. A lecithin:sphingomyelin ratio above 2
AnswerC. A lecithin:sphingomyelin ratio above 2
Lecithin (surfactant) rises late in gestation while sphingomyelin stays flat; an L:S ratio above 2 means enough surfactant, so the lungs are mature.
Explanation

Fetal lung fluid drains into the amniotic fluid, so amniocentesis samples the surfactant the lungs are making. Sphingomyelin serves as a steady reference. When the lecithin:sphingomyelin (L:S) ratio exceeds 2, the risk of RDS is low.

  • Below 2 means immature lungs and a high risk of RDS: the trigger for giving the mother corticosteroids if delivery cannot be delayed.
  • S:L above 2 is the ratio upside down (L:S below 0.5), a very immature result.

The pleura and the pleural cavity

L45Learning
How is the thoracic cavity divided into compartments?
  1. A. Two pleural cavities, one per lung, with the mediastinum between them
  2. B. Two pleural cavities, one per lung, with the diaphragm between them
  3. C. One pleural cavity around both lungs, with the mediastinum inside it
AnswerA. Two pleural cavities, one per lung, with the mediastinum between them
The thoracic cavity holds two separate pleural cavities (one per lung) with the mediastinum between them; the heart has its own sac, the pericardium.
Explanation

The thoracic cavity has three compartments: a right and a left pulmonary (pleural) cavity, each lined by its own pleural sac, and the central mediastinum, which holds the heart, great vessels, trachea and oesophagus. Each organ has a two-layered serous lining: pleura for the lungs, pericardium for the heart.

  • The diaphragm is the floor under both pleural cavities; it does not separate them from each other.
  • There is no single pleural cavity: the two sacs are completely separate, so air in one side (pneumothorax) does not collapse the other lung.

L56Learning
What can pleuritis leave behind, and what does it usually mean for breathing?
  1. A. Fibrous adhesions joining visceral to parietal pleura; once chronic they often have no functional consequence
  2. B. Fibrous adhesions joining visceral to parietal pleura; they make the lung collapse as the chest wall expands
  3. C. Fibrous adhesions joining parietal pleura to the ribs; they stop the ribs moving in breathing
AnswerA. Fibrous adhesions joining visceral to parietal pleura; once chronic they often have no functional consequence
Pleuritis roughens the pleura and can leave adhesions between the visceral and parietal layers; chronic adhesions often have no functional consequence.
Explanation

Inflamed pleura loses its smooth, lubricated surface: the layers grate (a pleural rub), and acute pleuritis gives sharp, stabbing pain, worse on exertion, because the parietal pleura is pain-sensitive. As the inflammation settles, fibrous tissue can bridge the pleural cavity and stick the visceral pleura to the parietal pleura. The lung then moves with the chest wall instead of sliding along it, so chronic adhesions often cause no symptoms and no loss of function.

  • Adhesions hold the lung to the wall; a lung collapses only when the two layers separate, as when air enters the cavity.
  • Adhesions form between the two pleural layers, inside the pleural cavity; they do not bind the pleura to the ribs, which keep moving.

AnswerC. Along the bronchi to the respiratory bronchioles, where they anastomose with pulmonary arteries

The bronchial arteries are small systemic branches of the thoracic aorta (on the right often by way of a posterior intercostal artery): one on the right, two on the left.
